Clio AI Investment Assistant

Dense financial data, made human. A case study in progressive disclosure, behavioural nudges, and designing for people who don’t speak the language of markets yet.

Role
Product Designer
Platform
Conceptual Web App
Duration
2 Weeks
Tools
Figma,

Overview

The brief in one sentence

Design a portfolio management feature for Clio that makes complex financial data understandable for novice investors without dumbing it down for experienced ones.

The challenge wasn’t purely visual. It was cognitive how do you show volatility, risk exposure, sector allocation, and smart trade recommendations to someone who opened their first investment account three weeks ago, without overwhelming them into inaction?

“When I open my portfolio I see a wall of numbers and feel like I’m missing something. I just close the app.”

The Problem

Information density is not the same as clarity

Most investment apps were designed for people who already understand finance. They cram every metric onto one screen because power users want it. But for everyone else, it triggers decision paralysis too much information, no action at all.

This isn’t a cosmetic problem. It’s a retention and trust problem. Users who feel confused churn. Users who feel empowered invest more and stay longer.

Key pain points

  • Dashboard shows 14+ data points simultaneously with no visual hierarchy
  • No contextual explanation of what numbers mean or what to do with them
  • Trade recommendations appear with no rationale, creating distrust
  • Risk levels displayed as jargonheavy scores (e.g.“Beta 1.4”)
  • No sense of progress or portfolio health at a glance

Research

Listening before designing

User Interviews

12 sessions with novice investors and 6 with experienced traders. Goal: map the knowledge gap and identify where confidence collapses.

Competitive Analysis

Evaluated 5 platforms including Zerodha, Groww, and Robinhood on information hierarchy, onboarding clarity, and trade decision support.

Cognitive Load Mapping

Taskbased test asking users to answer 5 simple questions using their current app. Measured timetoanswer and error rates to find overload points.

Key insight: novice users don’t need less data they need data presented with context and intent. They want to know what something meansfor them, not just what it is.

Design Process

From insight to interface

01

Define the user spectrum

Created two primary personas:“Curious Priya”(firsttime investor, wants simplicity) and“Confident Raj”(2+ years, wants density). The design needed to serve both without compromise.

02

Progressive disclosure architecture

Restructured information hierarchy so the default view shows only three highvalue signals: portfolio health score, today’s change, and a single action nudge. All detail is one tap away.

03

Smart Trade flows

Designed an AIpowered recommendation system that shows not just what to trade but why with plainlanguage rationale, risk profile alignment, and a“what happens if I do/don’t”framing.

04

Contextual data visualisation

Replaced raw charts with annotated visuals. Every significant data point has a tooltip in plain language. Sector allocation shows as a labelled visual map, not just percentages.

05

Usability testing and iteration

Two rounds of moderated testing with 8 participants using Maze. Iterated on the Smart Trade confirmation flow after discovering users wanted one more step of reassurance before committing.

What this project taught me

Simplicity is not reduction

Hiding information is not the same as designing for clarity. Progressive disclosure works because it respects the user’s journey giving them what they need, when they need it.

Quantum Virtual Classroom

A virtual classroom experience integrated into Quantum LMS that helps teachers organise live lessons, connect Zoom sessions, manage attendance and participation, record classes, and make replays easy for students to find.

Role
Senior UI Designer
Platform
Quantum LMS Web App
Duration
6 Months
Focus
Teacher Workflow + Live Learning

Overview

The brief in one sentence

Design a virtual classroom feature inside Quantum LMS that gives teachers one reliable place to plan, launch, manage, record, and review live online classes.

The challenge was connecting the flexibility of Zoom with the structure of an LMS. Teachers needed to organise sessions without duplicating work, while students needed a clear route to join live classes and watch recordings later.

“The live class should feel like part of the course, not a separate link teachers have to manage on the side.”

The Problem

Live teaching was fragmented across tools

Teachers had to move between the LMS, calendars, Zoom links, attendance records, and recording locations to run one class. That fragmentation made preparation slower and made it difficult to maintain a reliable history of what happened in each session.

Students also needed a predictable place to see upcoming classes, join on time, and find the recording after the session ended.

Key pain points

  • Teachers had to create and share Zoom links manually
  • Session scheduling was disconnected from course structure
  • Attendance and participation were difficult to manage consistently
  • Recordings were not clearly connected to the original class
  • Students had no single view for upcoming, live, and recorded sessions

Design Approach

One classroom workflow inside the LMS

Organise

Teachers create a classroom session from the course, add the topic and agenda, choose the date and time, and invite the right learners.

Connect

Zoom is integrated into the session workflow so teachers can launch the live class from Quantum without managing a separate link.

Continue

Attendance, recordings, notes, and replay access stay attached to the classroom session so learning can continue after the live event.

The design treats a virtual class as a complete learning object, not only as a video meeting. Planning, teaching, documentation, and review are connected in one LMS-native experience.

What changed

From meeting link to managed classroom

Session planning

Teachers can arrange classroom sessions around course topics, schedules, and learner groups instead of maintaining a separate calendar and link list.

Zoom launch and control

Live meeting access is connected to the classroom record, reducing setup friction and making the join action clear for both teachers and students.

Live class management

Attendance, participant status, announcements, and class notes are treated as part of the teaching workflow rather than manual follow-up work.

Recording and replay

Recorded sessions are linked back to the class so students can review missed content and teachers can keep a durable learning history.

Amazon Assure

Postpurchase anxiety is a silent churn driver. This feature case study tackles what happens after the buy button and why that moment defines longterm customer trust.

Role
Lead UX Designer
Platform
iOS, Android, Web
Duration
6 Weeks
Tools
Figma, Hotjar, Lookback

Overview

The brief in one sentence

Design a trustbuilding feature for Amazon that reduces postpurchase anxiety, lowers WISMO (Where Is My Order) contact rates, and improves customer satisfaction without adding friction to the existing checkout flow.

“I bought it three days ago. I just keep refreshing the tracking page every hour hoping something has changed.”

The Problem

The experience falls apart after the sale

The invisible problem with ecommerce is that the experience falls apart the moment the customer pays. The product is sold. The design team moves on. But the user is now in a state of uncertainty that can last days and that uncertainty is where trust is won or lost.

Key pain points

  • Return initiation requires 5+ steps with confusing policy language at each point
  • Order tracking shows carrier codes and depot names with no plainlanguage status
  • No proactive communication when delays occur
  • No visual confirmation of what the guarantee covers until a problem arises
  • Customer support chat used mostly for questions the UI could answer

Research

Finding the trust gaps

Customer Journey Mapping

Mapped the postpurchase journey for 20 users from order confirmation to receipt, identifying every moment of uncertainty and every touch point that either built or eroded trust.

Review Mining

Analysed 500 onestar reviews across product categories. 60% mentioned order uncertainty, return difficulty, or lack of communication as the primary complaint.

Competitive Benchmarking

Evaluated return and tracking UX across Flipkart, Myntra, and Nykaa to identify what the market was doing well and where Amazon had an opportunity to lead.

Core insight: users didn’t need faster delivery. They needed fewer moments of uncertainty. Every step that told a user“here’s where your order is and what happens next”reduced anxiety, even if the timeline didn’t change.

Design Process

From insight to interface

01

Map the trust gaps first

Mapped all postconfirmation touch points on a trust scale before designing a single screen. This focused effort on the highestanxiety moments, not the most visible ones.

02

Design the guarantee badge system

A persistent visual badge communicating what protection the customer has, visible on order confirmation, tracking, and product pages.

03

Redesign the tracking page

Replaced carrier codes and depot names with a plainlanguage progress narrative:“Your order left the sorting facility in Pune and is on its way to Mumbai.”

04

Proactive delay communication

Inapp notification system that preempts customer queries by proactively communicating delays with reason, updated ETA, and a onetap support option.

05

Streamlined return initiation

Reduced return initiation from 5 steps to 2 with plainlanguage policy summaries at the decision point.

The experience lives after the sale

Tryfit Shopping App

TryFit brings the confidence of an in-store trial to online fashion. Users schedule delivery for up to three items, try them at home while the delivery partner waits, and purchase or return them immediately.

Role
UX Designer and Researcher
Platform
iOS Mobile App
Duration
6 Weeks
Focus
Research, IA, Wireframes, Prototyping

Overview

The brief in one sentence

Design a try-before-you-buy mobile service that lets shoppers schedule a convenient delivery, try up to three clothing items at home, and make an immediate purchase or return decision with the delivery partner present.

“I want to know the clothes fit before I commit, without going through another long return process.”

The Problem

Online fashion asks for commitment before confidence

Online clothing shoppers cannot physically inspect fit, fabric, or product quality before purchase. Uncertain sizing, products that do not match their online representation, and limited return options create dissatisfaction and delay the purchase decision.

TryFit addresses this by combining online convenience with an at-home trial: shoppers choose a delivery slot, try the items immediately, and complete or reverse the purchase at the doorstep.

Key pain points

  • Uncertainty about size and fit before ordering
  • Products may not match their online representation
  • Return processes are long and inconvenient
  • Some vendors provide limited or no return options
  • Customers cannot easily try multiple items before committing

Research

Listening before designing

Qualitative Research

In-depth interviews and focus groups explored sizing uncertainty, product mismatch, return frustrations, and how shoppers would use a try-before-you-buy service.

Quantitative Survey

Survey responses tested the appeal of scheduled delivery and instant returns. 90% liked the core concept, while hygiene was the main concern raised.

Personas and Journey

Personas for Tejas Jadhav and Arjun Patel clarified the needs of busy online shoppers: reliable fit, flexible delivery, accurate product representation, and efficient returns.

The research showed that the strongest opportunity was not another size chart. It was reducing commitment risk through a flexible delivery slot, a real try-on moment, and an immediate return path.

Personas

Two shoppers, one confidence problem

Tejas Jadhav, 24

Software Developer · Mumbai

Tejas shops online because her schedule is busy, but inconsistent sizing and long returns make every clothing order feel risky.

  • Goal: Try clothes at home and purchase only what fits perfectly
  • Frustrations: Inconsistent sizing, long returns, and inaccurate descriptions
  • Needs: Reliable fit, flexible delivery scheduling, and convenient returns

Arjun Patel, 30

DevOps Engineer · Mumbai

Arjun prefers online shopping for convenience and fashion variety, but often keeps items that do not fit because returning them takes too much effort.

  • Goal: Confirm fit before committing to a purchase
  • Frustrations: Difficult returns, product-quality mismatch, and limited time
  • Needs: Accurate product representation, efficient returns, and on-time delivery

Design Process

From research to a six-week product direction

The process combined design strategy, information architecture, empathy mapping, user flows, wireframes, visual design, prototyping, usability testing, and survey insights.

01

Empathize and define

Interview and survey findings were translated into a clear problem statement around fit uncertainty, return friction, and delayed purchase decisions.

02

Structure the shopping experience

Information architecture covered the menu, pincode and address setup, product listing, cart, offers, and the try-at-home journey.

03

Design the delivery choice

Users can choose a delivery time that fits their schedule, making the at-home trial practical for students, professionals, parents, and fashion enthusiasts.

04

Enable an immediate decision

The delivery experience supports real-time feedback: keep satisfactory items and return unsuitable items instantly while the delivery partner is present.

05

Prototype and test

Wireframes, visual design, prototyping, usability checks, and survey insights were iterated across the six-week timeline.

CodeVault Learn to Code

Interview prep is one of the most highstakes learning contexts that exists and most tools for it are terrible. CodeVault is the design response to a broken experience.

Role
Product Designer
Platform
iOS Mobile App
Duration
7 Weeks
Tools
Figma, Principle, UserTesting

Overview

The brief in one sentence

Design a mobile learning companion for developers preparing for technical interviews that fits into the real constraints of a working professional’s day commutes, lunch breaks, distracted evenings while still building deep, testable knowledge.

“I bought a course, got three hours in, and then work got busy. Now I feel too behind to start again.”

The Problem

Most prep tools were built for people with four hours a day

Most interview prep platforms were designed for people with four hours a day and a whiteboard. The reality is most candidates prep in fifteenminute windows between meetings, on their phones, with limited concentration. The design had to meet them there.

Key pain points

  • Longform video content can’t be consumed in short, interrupted sessions
  • No sense of daily progress the course feels the same length at 10% or 80%
  • Problems are presented without the contextual knowledge needed to solve them
  • No spaced repetition concepts learned are forgotten by interview day
  • Competitive leaderboards create anxiety rather than motivation

Research

Listening before designing

Learner Interviews

Spoke with 14 developers who had recently gone through interview prep. Mapped the emotional arc from initial motivation through the plateau phase where most people quit.

Learning Science Review

Researched spaced repetition, retrieval practice, and microlearning principles and identified which patterns from education research applied specifically to technical interview prep.

Platform Analysis

Evaluated LeetCode, AlgoExpert, Neetcode, and Grokking on engagement design, progress communication, and mobile experience quality.

Key insight: motivation to prep peaks at the start and collapses at“the plateau”around 3040% through, when easy problems are done and hard ones feel impossible. The design needed to address the plateau, not just the first session.

Design Process

From insight to interface

01

Daily tracks, not courses

Restructured content from“courses to complete”to“daily tracks to do.”Each track takes 1215 minutes and covers one concept endtoend. The sense of completion resets every day.

02

Spaced repetition engine

A review system that resurfaces concepts based on how confidently the user rated their understanding. Problems marked“shaky”come back in 24 hours. Problems marked“solid”come back in a week.

03

Contextual problem sets

Every problem is preceded by a 90second concept primer that gives just enough context to attempt it. No more arriving at a problem cold.

04

Streak and milestone design

Streak system that rewards consistency over volume. Missing one day breaks the streak but doesn’t reset progress a deliberate choice to reduce the“might as well quit”response.

05

Offlinefirst architecture

Problems, content, and progress sync when connected, but the core experience works on the tube, in a lift, and in areas with no signal.

Learnings

What this project taught me

Completion is an illusion

Long courses create a false finish line that makes any interruption feel like failure. Daily tracks reframe the goal: not“finish the course”but“show up today.”

The motivation collapse at 3040% is predictable and preventable. Designing for it specifically keeps users moving through the hardest stretch.

Confidence is a metric too

Offline is not an edge case

Treating connectivity as optional rather than required changed how we thought about the entire product architecture. Features that require a connection are features some users will never use.

MAI Labs Company Website

A design that doesn’t reach code is still just a hypothesis. This project was about closing the gap from Figma to deployed, fully responsible for both.

Role

Platform

Duration

Tools

Overview

The brief in one sentence

Design and develop the complete MAI Labs company website from the ground up fully responsive, performant across browsers, and built with a reusable component system that the internal team could maintain and extend after handoff.

“Every time we try to update the site, something else breaks. We’ve just stopped updating it.”

The Problem

A site the team can’t own

This was a different kind of design challenge. There was no user problem to diagnose there was a craft problem. The existing site was slow, visually inconsistent, and couldn’t be updated without breaking things. The brief was: build something the team can own.

Key pain points

  • No component system every page was customcoded, making updates expensive
  • No mobile experience layout broke below 1200px
  • Page load time over 6 seconds due to unoptimised assets
  • Contact and application forms sent emails to a personal inbox with no structure
  • Brand inconsistency across pages multiple type scales and button styles

Research

Understanding before building

Codebase Audit

Reviewed the existing site for architectural issues, dependency bloat, and performance bottlenecks. A full rebuild would be faster and more maintainable than attempting to refactor.

Stakeholder Interviews

Three sessions with the founding team to understand the site’s goals, the team’s technical comfort level for future maintenance, and the content they most needed to communicate.

Component Inventory

Mapped all UI patterns needed across the site to design a minimal, complete component library before writing a single line of production code.

Design Process

From insight to interface

01

Design the component system first

Before any page layout, designed and agreed on the complete component library: buttons, cards, navigation, forms, and typography tokens.

02

Build with Next.js App Router

Chose Next.js for serverside rendering, builtin image optimisation, and filebased routing that makes future page additions intuitive for nonengineers.

03

Tailwind utility system

Utilityfirst approach means any team member can read and update styles without understanding the full CSS architecture.

04

APIintegrated forms

Built contact and job application forms with serverside API routes, validation, and structured email templates. Applications arrive formatted and filterable.

05

Performance and crossbrowser QA

Lighthouse audits on every page. Compressed and converted all assets to WebP. Tested across Chrome, Safari, Firefox, and Edge on desktop and mobile.

AccessIQ WCAG Summariser

Accessibility audits are only useful if teams can read them. AccessIQ translates WCAG compliance findings into plain language closing the gap between audit and action.

Role
Designer & Developer
Platform
Web App
Duration
Side Project
Tools
Figma, React, OpenAI API, Tailwind CSS

Overview

The brief in one sentence

Build a tool that makes WCAG 2.1 compliance findings understandable for design and development teams who have the will to fix accessibility issues but lack the knowledge to interpret technical audit reports.

“We got an audit back with 47 issues. I read the first three, had no idea what they meant practically, and forwarded it to a developer who also had no idea.”

The Problem

It’s a translation problem, not a willingness problem

The accessibility problem in most organisations isn’t malice or laziness. It’s translation. Audit reports come back written in WCAG criterion language that means nothing to a designer who’s never read the spec. The findings sit in a spreadsheet and nothing changes.

Key pain points

  • WCAG criterion codes (e.g.“1.4.3 Contrast Minimum AA”) are meaningless without deep knowledge of the spec
  • Audit reports list issues without designspecific remediation guidance
  • No differentiation between issues affecting different user groups
  • No prioritisation of issues by severity and actual user impact
  • Designers and developers need different guidance for the same issue

Research

Understanding the gap

Designer Interviews

Spoke with 8 product designers at companies that had recently received accessibility audits. Mapped how reports were read, who they were forwarded to, and how many issues were actually resolved.

Specialist Consultation

Consulted with two certified accessibility specialists to understand which WCAG criteria are most commonly misunderstood, and what plainlanguage framing works best for each category.

Audit Report Analysis

Collected and analysed 12 real accessibility audit reports from opensource projects. Categorised findings by criterion type, severity, and how actionable the guidance was.

Design Process

From insight to interface

01

Plainlanguage criterion library

Built a complete library of all Level A and AA WCAG 2.1 criteria, each translated into three versions: a onesentence summary, a designerspecific guide, and a developerspecific fix guide.

02

AI interpretation layer

Integrated OpenAI’s API to take raw audit output and map findings to the criterion library, generating a structured, plainlanguage action plan automatically.

03

Rolebased remediation views

Separate output views for designers and developers. The same issue surfaces different guidance depending on who is reading it.

04

Severity and impact scoring

Custom severity model that scores each issue on both WCAG level and estimated user impact across disability categories, allowing teams to prioritise by realworld effect.

05

Shareable report output

Clean, exportable report format that product managers can share with stakeholders as a readable brief rather than a technical spreadsheet.
