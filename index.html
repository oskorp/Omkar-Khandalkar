Quantum Virtual Classroom

Turning a Zoom link into a managed classroom

A virtual classroom inside Quantum LMS that gives teachers one reliable place to plan, launch, manage, record and review live lessons.

Role
Senior Product Designer
Platform
Quantum LMS, web
Timeline
6 months
Focus
Teacher workflow, live learning

The problem

Running one live class meant moving between the LMS, a calendar, Zoom, attendance records and a recording location.

What I did

Designed the class as a single learning object inside the course, from planning through to replay.

The result

Five disconnected places became one class record, with one view of upcoming, live and recorded sessions for students.

My Contribution

What I was responsible for

  • Owned the end-to-end classroom experience for teachers and students
  • Worked with Product, Engineering and QA inside the Quantum delivery team
  • Applied the shared Quantum UI patterns and WCAG standards to the new workflow

The Problem

Live teaching was fragmented across tools

Teachers had to move between the LMS, calendars, Zoom links, attendance records and recording locations to run a single class. Preparation was slow, and there was no reliable history of what happened in each session.

Students had the mirror-image problem: no predictable place to see upcoming classes, join on time or find the recording afterwards.

Key pain points

  • Zoom links created and shared by hand
  • Scheduling disconnected from course structure
  • Attendance and participation hard to manage consistently
  • Recordings not connected to the original class
  • No single student view of upcoming, live and recorded sessions

“The live class should feel like part of the course, not a separate link teachers have to manage on the side.”

The Insight

Reframing the problem

Key insight

A virtual class is not a video meeting. It is a learning object with a before, a during and an after, and all three belong to the course.

Key Decisions

The calls that shaped the product

01

Make the class the unit of design, not the meeting

Teachers create a session from inside the course, with topic, agenda, time and learners attached.

Why
It removes duplicate setup and gives every class a home that outlives the call.
The trade-off
Teachers need a little more structure up front than pasting a link.
02

Launch Zoom from the class record

Zoom sits inside the session workflow, so teachers and students reach the call from Quantum.

Why
Join becomes one clear action for both roles and link management disappears.
The trade-off
The experience now depends on the integration, so failure states have to be designed as carefully as the happy path.
03

Treat attendance and notes as teaching, not admin

Attendance, participant status, announcements and class notes are part of running the class.

Why
Follow-up work moves into the moment it happens instead of being reconstructed later.
The trade-off
The live view has to hold more without distracting a teacher who is mid-lesson.
04

Attach recordings to the class that produced them

Replay lives on the class, alongside attendance and notes.

Why
Students who missed a lesson find it where they would expect, and teachers keep a durable learning history.
The trade-off
Recordings depend on processing time, so the session needs a clear in-between state.

The System

One class record replaces five places to look

Scope the object before the screens

Deciding that the class was the core object made the later decisions about Zoom, attendance and replay much simpler.

Most of the lasting value of a live class appears once the call ends, in the record it leaves behind.

Time to set up a class, on-time joins, and how often students return to replays are the signals I would track to prove the workflow is working.

Hiring a photographer in India usually means a phone number, a WhatsApp negotiation, and no price transparency or payment protection.

Defined the product from research to edge cases, then designed and directed the build of a connected booking product.

A working product for customers, creators and admins, with 30 of 40 edge cases fully covered. It runs on mock payments ahead of launch.

The Problem

Photographers are listed as leads, not bookings

On wedding platforms, a customer gets a phone number and then negotiates on WhatsApp, with no price transparency, no payment protection and no delivery guarantee.

Non-wedding shoots such as product, real estate, car delivery and drone work have no dedicated discovery channel at all, and run on Instagram DMs and word of mouth. Creators have problems too: paid leads that don’t convert, payment defaults and endless revision requests.

Key pain points

  • No visible prices, so every enquiry becomes a negotiation
  • No protection if a shoot is cancelled or never delivered
  • Reviews that are hard to trust
  • Commercial and drone shoots have no real marketplace
  • Creators pay for leads that often don’t convert

Research and Insight

What changed our direction

Market and demand

Mapped how Indian shoots really work: muhurat-date peaks, multi-event weddings, reels-first deliverables, car delivery shoots and drone licensing.

Competitive analysis

Compared WedMeGood, Weddingz.in, WeddingWire India, Phodio, JustDial, and the Instagram and WhatsApp route people actually use.

Creator pain points

Documented the supply side from desk research: unproductive leads, advance-payment habits, scope creep and clumsy large-file delivery.

Key insight

No Indian player combined all-category coverage, transparent packages, real availability and escrow. The opportunity was to make a shoot as bookable as a hotel room.

Key Decisions

The calls that shaped the product

01

Sell bookings, not leads

Creators publish structured packages with visible starting prices, real availability, and instant or request-to-book.

Why
Customers can compare and commit without starting a negotiation.
The trade-off
Creators have to fit their work into packages up front, which is harder for bespoke shoots.
02

Model advance culture with milestone escrow

Payment is split across advance, before shoot and on delivery, and held until each stage is released.

Why
Creators in India commonly take advances, so escrow had to work with that habit rather than against it.
The trade-off
Every stage needs its own rules for cancellations, disputes and payouts, and launch needs a licensed payment partner.
03

Make trust visible

Badges show KYC, GST and DGCA drone certification, and only completed bookings can leave reviews.

Why
When money is paid before a shoot, people need proof, not promises.
The trade-off
Verification lengthens creator onboarding and requires a real moderation operation.
04

Treat edge cases as the design spec

40 scenarios, such as double booking, debited-but-failed payments and an SOS during a shoot, each needed a UI state, a rule and a test.

Why
In a marketplace holding money, the unhappy paths are where trust is won or lost.
The trade-off
Depth came before breadth, so features like Instagram import were held back.
05

Design for Indian phones and Indian context first

Screens start at 360px, with Hindi and English, rupee formatting, IST times and muhurat dates built in. Native form controls keep first-load JavaScript low.

Why
Most customers will arrive on a phone, often outside the big metros.
The trade-off
Native controls give up some custom interaction polish.

The System

One booking, three connected workspaces

Every booking status change goes through a single state machine, so customers, creators and admins always see the same truth. Money is stored as integer paise, and times are stored in UTC and shown in IST.

Clio AI Investment Assistant

Making dense financial data understandable for first-time investors

A portfolio feature that serves novices without dumbing things down for experienced traders, using progressive disclosure and explainable AI recommendations.

Role
Product Designer
Platform
Conceptual web app
Timeline
2 weeks
Tools
Figma, Google Forms, Google Meet, Maze

The problem

Portfolio dashboards show every metric at once. New investors feel overwhelmed and close the app.

What I did

Rebuilt the hierarchy around three signals and designed AI trade suggestions that explain themselves.

The result

In usability testing, task completion rose from 54% to 89% and comprehension improved by 40%.

My Contribution

What I was responsible for

  • Handled research, interaction design and visual design
  • Ran interviews, a competitive review and usability tests
  • Designed the portfolio view, health score and Smart Trade flows

The Problem

Information density is not the same as clarity

Most investment apps are built for people who already understand finance. Every metric lands on one screen because power users want it, and everyone else freezes.

That makes this a trust and retention problem as much as a visual one. How do you show volatility, risk and allocation to someone who opened an account three weeks ago, without overwhelming them into doing nothing?

Key pain points

  • 14+ data points shown at once with no hierarchy
  • No explanation of what numbers mean or what to do
  • Trade recommendations with no rationale
  • Risk shown as jargon, such as “Beta 1.4”
  • No sense of portfolio health at a glance

“When I open my portfolio I see a wall of numbers and feel like I’m missing something. I just close the app.”

Research and Insight

What changed our direction

User interviews

12 sessions with novice investors and 6 with experienced traders, to find where confidence collapses.

Competitive analysis

5 platforms including Zerodha, Groww and Robinhood, compared on hierarchy, onboarding and decision support.

Cognitive load mapping

Users answered 5 simple questions in their current app, while I measured time to answer and errors.

Key insight

Novice investors don’t need less data. They need data with context and intent: what it means for them, not just what it is.

Key Decisions

The calls that shaped the product

01

Lead with three signals

The default view shows portfolio health, today’s change and one suggested action. Everything else is one tap away.

Why
It answers “am I okay, and what should I do?” before asking anyone to read a chart.
The trade-off
Experienced investors take one extra tap to reach detail, so depth had to stay close by.
02

Make AI recommendations show their work

Smart Trade explains why, in plain language, with risk-profile fit and a “what happens if I do or don’t” framing.

Why
People won’t act on a suggestion they don’t understand, however accurate it is.
The trade-off
Explanations add reading time to a decision that could be one tap.
03

Add a reassurance step after testing

Testing showed users wanted one more moment of confirmation before committing, so the flow gained a step.

Why
With real money at stake, confidence matters more than speed.
The trade-off
A deliberately longer flow, which goes against the usual instinct to remove steps.
04

Annotate the data itself

Key data points carry plain-language tooltips, and sector allocation is a labelled map.

Why
Learning happens in context rather than in a separate help section.
The trade-off
Annotations need careful density control so they don’t recreate the clutter.

Online fashion asks for commitment before confidence

Shoppers can’t inspect fit, fabric or quality before paying. Uncertain sizing, products that don’t match their photos, and limited returns all delay the decision or leave people disappointed.

Some shoppers even keep items that don’t fit, because returning them is too much effort.

Key pain points

  • Uncertainty about size and fit before ordering
  • Products that don’t match how they look online
  • Long, inconvenient return processes
  • Vendors with limited or no return options
  • No easy way to try several items before committing

“I want to know the clothes fit before I commit, without going through another long return process.”

Interviews and focus groups

Explored sizing uncertainty, product mismatch, return frustration and how people would use a try-before-you-buy service.

It keeps the doorstep moment short enough for a delivery partner to wait.

Shoppers comparing many options may need more than one delivery.

Keep what fits and return the rest while the delivery partner is present.

Why
It removes packaging, drop-offs and waiting from the return journey. 76% said this would improve shopping.

The decision happens under some time pressure.

The Problem

Most prep tools were built for people with four hours a day

Most platforms assume long sessions and a whiteboard. In reality, candidates prepare in fifteen-minute gaps between meetings, on their phones, with limited concentration.

The design had to meet them there, while still building knowledge that lasts until the interview.

Key pain points

  • No sense of daily progress

Learner interviews

14 developers who had recently prepared for interviews, mapping motivation from start to plateau.

Learning science review

Spaced repetition, retrieval practice and microlearning, applied to interview prep.

Platform analysis

LeetCode, AlgoExpert, NeetCode and Grokking, on engagement and mobile quality.

Motivation collapses at a predictable point, about 30 to 40% in, when easy problems are done and hard ones feel impossible. The design had to solve the plateau, not just the first session.

The trade-off

Missing a day breaks the streak but doesn’t reset progress.

Why
It deliberately avoids the “might as well quit” response.

AccessIQ WCAG Guide

Making all of WCAG 2.2 readable for the people who have to apply it

A free, interactive guide that pairs every WCAG success criterion with a plain description and a concrete failure and fix.

Role
Designer and developer
Platform
Responsive web
Type
Side project, CodeOS
Tools
Figma, HTML, JavaScript, Tailwind CSS

The problem

Teams want to fix accessibility issues, but WCAG is written in specification language most designers and developers can't act on.

What I did

Designed and built a searchable reference that turns each criterion into a description, a bad example and a good one.

The result

A live guide covering 83 success criteria across Levels A, AA and AAA, including the WCAG 2.2 additions.

My Contribution

What I was responsible for

  • Designed and developed the guide from concept to deployment
  • Wrote and structured the content for all 83 criteria
  • Built the responsive interface, navigation and light and dark themes

The Problem

It is a translation problem, not a willingness problem

Accessibility rarely fails because people don't care. It fails at translation. Findings arrive in criterion language that means little to a designer who has never read the specification, and then they sit in a spreadsheet.

The specification is precise, but precision is not the same as usable. What teams need at the moment of fixing something is a picture of what wrong and right actually look like.

Key pain points

  • Criterion codes mean little without deep knowledge of the spec
  • Guidance is written for compliance, not for making a change
  • Examples are scattered across long documents
  • Hard to tell quickly which conformance level applies
  • New WCAG 2.2 criteria are easy to miss

“We got an audit back with 47 issues. I read the first three, had no idea what they meant practically, and forwarded it to a developer who also had no idea.”

Research and Insight

What changed our direction

Designer interviews

Spoke with 8 product designers whose companies had recently received accessibility audits, mapping how reports were read and passed around.

Specialist consultation

Consulted two certified accessibility specialists on which criteria are most often misunderstood.

Audit report analysis

Analysed 12 accessibility audit reports from open-source projects by criterion, severity and how actionable the guidance was.

Key insight

People don't need WCAG summarised. They need to see a failure and a fix side by side.

Key Decisions

The calls that shaped the product

01

Keep the WCAG structure as the backbone

The guide follows the four principles and the official criterion numbers, with a persistent table of contents.

Why
Audit reports cite criterion numbers, so a reader has to be able to look one up directly.
The trade-off
It is less friendly for browsing by task, such as forms or media.
02

Give every criterion a failure and a fix

All 83 entries carry a plain description, a bad example and a good example.

Why
Comparison teaches faster than a definition, and it gives designers and developers a shared reference.
The trade-off
Examples simplify. Real interfaces can fail a criterion in ways one example can't cover.
03

Show code only where code clarifies

26 entries include code. The rest stay in plain language.

Why
Criteria about media or content are better understood as scenarios, and it keeps the guide useful to designers.
The trade-off
Developers get less copy-paste guidance on some criteria.
04

Include AAA and flag what is new

Level badges appear on every criterion, and WCAG 2.2 additions are clearly marked.

Why
Teams can scope to their target level at a glance and catch up on new requirements.
The trade-off
More entries make the list longer, so navigation has to carry more weight.

Outcome

A reference teams can open and use today

What I took from this

Accessibility is a communication problem

MAI Labs Company Website

Rebuilding a company site the team could finally own

Designed and developed end to end, around a reusable component system the internal team could maintain after handoff.

Role

Platform

Timeline

The problem

The old site was slow, inconsistent, broke on mobile and couldn’t be updated without something else breaking.

What I did

Designed a component system first, then built the full site in Next.js with structured forms.

The result

Lighthouse performance rose from 41 to 94, and the team could update content without touching code.

My Contribution

What I was responsible for

  • Responsible for design and front-end development
  • Ran a codebase audit and three stakeholder sessions
  • Built the component library, forms and handoff documentation

The Problem

A site the team couldn’t own

The existing site was slow, visually inconsistent and fragile. The team had stopped updating it because every change broke something else.

The brief wasn’t just a better-looking website. It was a site the team could keep running without me.

Key pain points

  • No component system, so every page was custom-coded
  • Layout broke below 1200px
  • Page load over 6 seconds from unoptimised assets
  • Forms sent unstructured email to a personal inbox
  • Multiple type scales and button styles across pages

“Every time we try to update the site, something else breaks. We’ve just stopped updating it.”

Research and Insight

What changed our direction

Codebase audit

Reviewed architecture, dependencies and performance bottlenecks.

Stakeholder interviews

Three sessions with the founding team on goals, content and technical comfort.

Component inventory

Mapped every UI pattern needed before writing production code.

Key insight

The real user of this project was the person maintaining it after I left.

Key Decisions

The calls that shaped the product

01

Rebuild rather than refactor

The audit showed a full rebuild would be faster and more maintainable.

Why
Fixing a site with no shared components would have preserved the underlying problem.
The trade-off
No incremental wins along the way, so stakeholders had to trust the plan until launch.
02

Agree the component system before any page

Buttons, cards, navigation, forms and type tokens were designed and signed off first.

Why
A week of system work saved around three weeks of inconsistency fixes later.
The trade-off
Early progress looks slow, because there are no finished pages to show.
03

Choose Next.js and Tailwind for maintainers

Server rendering, image optimisation and file-based routing, with utility classes anyone can read.

Why
Adding a page stays simple, and styles can be changed without knowing the whole CSS architecture.
The trade-off
Utility-heavy markup is more verbose.
04

Turn forms into a workflow

Contact and job application forms use server-side validation and structured templates.

Why
Applications arrive formatted and filterable instead of landing in one person’s inbox.
The trade-off
More server-side logic to maintain than a simple form link.

Outcome

Faster, and independent after handoff

What I took from this

Maintainability is a design output

A site a team can’t update eventually stops being updated.

Component-first is faster, not slower

Performance is a feature

Document for the person after you

Handoff notes explained why decisions were made, not only how to add a page.
